Important Caveats & Limitations

This dashboard synthesises data from multiple peer-reviewed sources and international organisations. Users should be aware of the following limitations:

  • Regional aggregation: Impact values represent region-wide medians. Within any region, impacts vary enormously by country, micro-climate, soil type, altitude, water access, and farming system. These figures should not be used for local or national planning.
  • CO₂ fertilisation uncertainty: Elevated CO₂ can boost C3 crop photosynthesis (wheat, rice, soybeans) by 10–20% under ideal conditions, partially offsetting heat/drought losses. However, CO₂ benefits are reduced under nutrient/water limitation and may decrease nutritional quality. The yield-change values shown here represent net median estimates that partially incorporate CO₂ fertilisation, but the magnitude of this effect remains actively debated.
  • Adaptation not fully modelled: Impact projections assume limited autonomous adaptation (e.g., modest planting date shifts). They do not model transformative adaptation such as large-scale irrigation expansion, major breeding breakthroughs, or wholesale relocation of farming systems. With strong adaptation, actual losses could be significantly smaller.
  • Livestock impact estimates are synthesised: Unlike crop yield projections (which draw on large meta-analyses), livestock productivity impacts are constructed from Temperature-Humidity Index (THI) research, feed-crop projections, and IPCC qualitative assessments. The specific percentage values are informed estimates, not outputs of a single quantitative model.
  • Warming scenarios are simplified: Real-world warming is not uniform. Land warms faster than oceans, high latitudes faster than the tropics, and nighttime temperatures faster than daytime. A "+2°C global mean" may correspond to +3–4°C in continental interiors.
  • The +1°C scenario reflects the recent past: Global mean temperature surpassed +1°C above pre-industrial around 2017. As of 2024, warming stands at approximately +1.2°C, with temporary excursions to ~+1.5°C. The +1°C data point shows impacts that are already occurring, not a future projection.
  • Production figures are approximate: Global production volumes are rounded estimates from FAO/USDA data and fluctuate annually with weather, trade, and market conditions. Figures marked with "~" are rounded to the nearest significant figure.
  • Non-linear and compound risks: At higher warming levels (+3°C and above), impacts may be non-linear due to tipping points, compound extremes (simultaneous heat + drought + flooding), and cascading failures across food systems. The linear interpolation between data points may underestimate tail risks.

Crop Yield Impact Projections

Regional yield-change arrays are derived from meta-analyses cited in IPCC AR6 WGII Chapter 5, IPCC SRCCL Figure 5.2, and the studies below. Values represent median estimates across multiple climate models and crop models (typically CMIP6 x GGCMI ensembles). Ranges in the underlying literature are wide; the single values shown here are central tendencies.

  • [Zhao2017] Zhao, C. et al. (2017). Temperature increase reduces global yields of major crops. PNAS, 114(35), 9326–9331. Key finding: Each degree of warming reduces global yields of wheat by 6.0%, rice by 3.2%, maize by 7.4%, and soybean by 3.1%.
  • [Asseng2015] Asseng, S. et al. (2015). Rising temperatures reduce global wheat production. Nature Climate Change, 5, 143–147. Key finding: Multi-model ensemble projects median wheat yield decline of 6% per degree C of warming.
  • [Challinor2014] Challinor, A.J. et al. (2014). A meta-analysis of crop yield under climate change and adaptation. Nature Climate Change, 4, 287–291. Key finding: Adaptation measures offset some losses, but tropical regions remain net negative.
  • [Ray2019] Ray, D.K. et al. (2019). Climate change has likely already affected global food production. PLOS ONE, 14(5), e0217148. Key finding: Climate trends since 1974 have already reduced global consumable food calories by ~1%.
  • [Jagermeyr2021] Jägermeyr, J. et al. (2021). Climate impacts on global agriculture emerge earlier in new generation of climate and crop models. Nature Food, 2, 873–885. Key finding: GGCMI Phase 3 ensemble shows maize yields declining 24% by end-of-century under SSP5-8.5, with earlier emergence of impacts than previously projected.
  • [Rosenzweig2014] Rosenzweig, C. et al. (2014). Assessing agricultural risks of climate change in the 21st century. PNAS, 111(9), 3268–3273.

Methodology Notes

How yield-change values were derived

The five-element arrays [+1°C, +2°C, +3°C, +4°C, +5°C] for each crop–region pair were constructed by:

  1. Starting with median values from IPCC AR6 WGII Chapter 5 meta-analyses and SRCCL Figure 5.2 (which cover +1–3°C well).
  2. Extending to +4–5°C using GGCMI ensemble results (Jägermeyr et al. 2021) and polynomial extrapolation from Zhao et al. (2017).
  3. Adjusting regionally using Challinor et al. (2014) tropical vs. temperate differentials and Rosenzweig et al. (2014) gridded estimates.
  4. Incorporating a partial CO₂ fertilisation benefit for C3 crops (wheat, rice, soybeans) of approximately +5–10% at elevated CO₂, dampened by nutrient and water limitations.

These are central estimates with substantial uncertainty. The 5th–95th percentile range around any given value is typically ±50–100% of the median (e.g., a median of −10% could range from −5% to −20%). Higher warming levels carry larger uncertainty bands.

How livestock impact values were derived

Livestock percentage impacts are synthesised estimates, not outputs of a single quantitative model. They were constructed from:

  1. Temperature-Humidity Index (THI) dose-response relationships for each species from animal science literature.
  2. IPCC AR6 WGII and SRCCL qualitative assessments of livestock vulnerability by region.
  3. Feed-crop yield projections (since livestock productivity depends heavily on feed availability).
  4. Regional climate projections for temperature and humidity changes under each warming level.

Users should treat livestock impact values as order-of-magnitude guidance rather than precise predictions.

Production shift arrows

The directional arrows on the map indicate well-documented or projected shifts in production suitability zones, based on crop-climate modelling studies cited above. They represent the direction of suitability shifts, not exact migration paths. Actual production shifts depend on land availability, policy, infrastructure, and market forces.
